I tested Goodfire's idea about Inkling 2 Mini's loop

What its biggest activation actually does

November 2026

Inkling 2 Mini runs its middle twelve layers three times per token, and each later pass reads the earlier one through a learned choice between two block outputs. Goodfire observed that one coordinate of the state, d2027 (called slot 2027 below), is enormous at the end of passes 1 and 2, where it holds 46% of the squared norm after pass 1, and collapses in pass 3. They suggested that it tells later passes which block to read. They reported no intervention, so I ran one. Their measurements reproduce, but the coordinate does not steer the read: hiding it shifts the reading mix by 1 to 3 points, and hiding the five largest contributors leaves 96% to 99% of layer-13 attention on block 3. The coordinate matters as a constant. Zeroing it changes about 10% of next-token predictions, while freezing it at its mean changes nothing measurable, and the damage arises at the gate that admits the between-pass message. A second, smaller test finds that removing the harmful-versus-harmless direction removes the refusal phrase only in pass 3, where 48 refusals fall to 16, and that this does not carry over to the variable-loop sibling model.

Part 1: Is slot 2027 a "read me" tag? A causal test

Background

The architecture, as implemented

Layer numbers in this post are 1-indexed to match Goodfire's post.

  • 24 layers in 4 blocks of 6. Blocks 2 and 3 (layers 7–18) run three times with shared weights. Blocks 1 and 4 run once.
  • There is no single residual stream. Each block keeps its own running state, and each layer builds its input by attending over the block outputs written so far plus its own running state. I call this the within-pass read.
  • Passes 2 and 3 each restart from the state at the end of block 1. They receive the previous pass only through a between-pass read. For each of the 12 repeated layers there are 4 learned queries (for the attention query, key and value inputs and for the MLP input). Each query takes a softmax over exactly two items from the previous pass: B, the output of block 2, and C, the output of block 3. Both are RMS-normalised first. The result is called the message.
  • The message enters the layer through a gate: out = (1 − forget) × h + gain × message, where forget and gain are computed from the normalised state and the normalised message.

So "which block does a later pass read" is, concretely, a two-way softmax between B and C of the previous pass, computed 48 times per pass.

What Goodfire reports

From https://www.goodfire.com/research/interpreting-inkling-2-mini (Lee, Krishnamurthy, Merullo, Balsam and Lubana, 2026-11-20).

Their terms map onto mine as follows: "depth-wise attention" is the within-pass read, "cross-loop attention" is the between-pass read, the "cross-loop readers" u_Q, u_V, u_K, u_MoE are the four learned queries per repeated layer, and their "keep" term is one minus the forget term of the gate.

  • The outlier. After RMS normalisation, slot 2027 has "an average RMS-normalized value of +32.4, occupying 46% of the activation's total squared norm". It is introduced as "one example of an outlier", the one with "the most anomalous magnitude".
  • When it is written. Their Figure 7 shows the components writing to the slot. Layer 18 writes a large positive spike in loops 1 and 2 and a large negative spike in loop 3.
  • Which block is read. In loops 2 and 3 the cross-loop attention goes mostly to block 3 of the previous loop, and block 3 "almost always attends to itself from its previous loop".
  • The gap decomposition. For layer 13 in the second loop, slot 2027 is "the largest (or 2nd largest) contributor" to the block-3-minus-block-2 score of the four readers, "contributing 17%, 19%, 11%, and 6% respectively on its own". They add that it "is not the only outlier": the top 5 coordinates account for 60%, 62%, 32% and 30%, and all five are among the 15 largest coordinates.
  • Weight-space evidence. MoE output weights that align with reader weights (absolute cosine above 0.30) are concentrated in the last layers of each block, which they interpret as those layers "tagging" activations so that later layers, or later loops, know which block to read. This is stated for depth-wise attention as well as for cross-loop attention.
  • Interpretation, in their words. The outliers "seem to determine which blocks the model should attend to across loops" and "play a role in facilitating information flow between loops". "We believe d2027 is used across loops to coordinate which blocks the future loops should read from." Of the loop-3 drop: "Our speculation is that because cross-loop communications are no longer needed, the model removes the activity on d2027."
  • No intervention is reported. The evidence is activation sizes, attention patterns, weight alignment and the gap decomposition. They describe the results as early.

Thinking Machines' release post (https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/) adds one relevant detail about the gate: it "initially blocks the entire message" and training increases the gain on the message over time.

Setup

  • Model: Inkling 2 Mini, as released on Hugging Face (thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini). The checkpoint's NVFP4 and MXFP8 expert weights are dequantised to bf16 on load. One A100 80GB.
  • Text: FineWeb (the sample-10BT subset), the first 600 documents with at least 1,500 characters. Each is tokenised with no chat template and no special tokens, and cut to the first 128 tokens.
  • Main experiment: 200 documents, 25,600 tokens, batch size 8. Position 0 is excluded from attention and activation statistics.
  • Instrumentation: the between-pass read is replaced by a function that performs the same operations in the same order and can additionally record or edit. With no edit the model's logits are bit-identical to the unpatched model.
  • Interventions act only on the between-pass read. "Routing" edits change the keys the softmax sees and leave the values that are mixed untouched. "Content" edits change the values and leave the keys untouched.
  • Metrics, all against the unedited model on the same tokens:
    • C share: mean attention on C in the between-pass read, averaged over the 4 query types, for block-2 layers (7–12) and block-3 layers (13–18).
    • loss: mean next-token cross-entropy.
    • KL: mean KL divergence from the unedited model's next-token distribution.
    • same top-1: share of positions whose most likely next token is unchanged.

Noise floor

This model amplifies tiny numerical differences. An early version of my patch computed the same sum in a different order; the float rounding difference alone changed 1.5% of top-1 predictions. Each Part 1 table of output effects therefore includes a noise-only row, where the values are jittered by Gaussian noise of relative size 1e-6. Effects near that row should be read as no effect.

Two things are visible before any intervention:

  • Slot 2027 is usually the single largest contributor in pass 2, but it supplies a minority of the gap.

Notes on the interventions:

  • "Hide" zeroes the slot in the normalised keys of both B and C. In a two-way softmax this removes exactly that slot's term from the gap.
  • "Most influential slots" are ranked by mean absolute contribution to the gap across all 48 queries and both passes. The top ten are 2027, 2110, 924, 378, 1698, 2230, 2117, 808, 943, 1886.
  • "Dataset mean" uses the mean of the normalised slot over all tokens, separately for B and C and for each pass (pass 2: B +22.5, C +32.6; pass 3: B +26.0, C +30.1).
  • Slots 2230 and 378 are the next-largest slots in B and C. The random slots were drawn with a fixed seed.

What the table shows

  1. The routing effect of slot 2027 is small. In pass 2, hiding it lowers the C share from 0.76 to 0.73 in block-2 layers and from 0.92 to 0.90 in block-3 layers. In pass 3 the change is 0.01. KL is 0.0020 against a noise floor of 0.0009.
  2. Moving the tag does not move the read. Swapping the slot between B and C, which reverses its contribution, lowers the pass-2 C share only to 0.70 / 0.88.
  3. The preference is distributed. Hiding the 100 most influential slots leaves the pass-2 C share at 0.63 / 0.77.
  4. The slot matters as content. Zeroing it in the values gives KL 0.0367 and changes 9.5% of top-1 predictions. The next-largest slots give KL 0.0106 and 0.0070; random slots sit at the noise floor. The loss increase, +0.0193, is about 3% of the increase from removing the message altogether (+0.6514).
  5. A constant does the job. Setting the slot to its dataset mean gives KL 0.0013, indistinguishable from noise.
  6. Reading only C is tolerated but not free. Forcing all attention onto C gives KL 0.0521; forcing it onto B is about as damaging as removing the message. This is an inference-time edit of a model trained with a free choice. It says nothing about a model trained with the read hard-wired.

Interpretation

  • Goodfire's measurements and their decomposition reproduce. What does not hold up is the functional reading that the outlier coordinates, slot 2027 or the top five together, determine which block later passes read.
  • Their own decomposition already implies the limit: a slot that supplies 6–19% of a 4–13 logit margin cannot control a softmax that is this far from its decision boundary.
  • The weaker statement, that the slot "plays a role" in between-pass information flow, is supported in a different sense from the one proposed. The next pass is sensitive to the slot being present in the message, and insensitive to its exact value.
  • This is consistent with the published account of massive activations in ordinary transformers as near-constant bias terms (Sun et al., 2024, "Massive Activations in Large Language Models"). I have not tested that account directly here.
  • The pass-3 collapse (C3 has slot 2027 at about −6) is real and unexplained by my tests. C3 is never read by a later pass, so the collapse cannot be probed through the between-pass read.

Follow-up A: does slot 2027 steer the within-pass reads?

Result 2 only covers the between-pass read. Every layer also builds its input by attending over the items available within the pass: the token embedding, the block outputs written so far, and its own running state. Slot 2027 differs a lot between those items, so it could act as a tag there. I test this on the same 200 documents.

Reading

  • Slot 2027 has more leverage on the within-pass reads of the repeated layers than on the between-pass read: the mix moves by about 6–8 points on average, against 1.6 points between passes, and a quarter of token-reads move by more than 10 points.
  • The mechanism is visible in the second table. In the repeated layers the layer's own running state carries the slot at about +20 to +30, while block 1's output and the token embedding carry it at about −6 and −3. I measured the size of the shift, not its direction.
  • The behavioural effect is real and small: 94.5% of top-1 predictions unchanged against a noise floor of 97.8%, KL 0.0135 against 0.0025, loss +0.0043. It comes entirely from the repeated layers.
  • This is in the direction of Goodfire's weight-space observation that the last layers of each block "tag" activations for the depth-wise readers. The measured effect of any one slot is small.
  • It is not specific to slot 2027. Hiding slot 1943 gives 95.1% and KL 0.0110. Slot 2230 does the same to a lesser degree, and slot 378 is at the noise floor.
  • Hiding slot 2027 from every read at once is no worse than hiding it from the within-pass reads alone, which again shows the between-pass read does not depend on it.

Follow-up B: why does removing slot 2027 from the message hurt?

Result 2 found that zeroing the slot in the message values changes about 10% of top-1 predictions, while freezing it at its mean changes nothing. One candidate explanation is rescaling: the slot holds about 46% of the squared norm, so once it is gone every normalisation downstream makes all the other slots look bigger. The experiment below separates the effects.

The message enters each repeated layer through a gate, out = (1 − forget) × h + gain × message, where forget and gain are computed from the normalised state and the normalised message, and out is RMS-normalised before the layer uses it. Removing the slot from the message has these parts:

  • G1: the gate no longer sees the slot's own value. Other slots are as in the clean run.
  • G2: the gate sees every other slot inflated. The slot itself is as in the clean run. This is the pure rescaling effect at the gate.
  • G3: the slot's message term is missing from the gate's output. This splits into G3a, the slot's own value after normalisation is lower with other slots as in the clean run, and G3b, every other slot is inflated after normalisation with the slot itself as in the clean run.

G1 + G2 + G3 together are identical to zeroing the slot in the message. G1, G2, G3a and G3b are implemented by patching the gate and the following RMS norm; with no edit the patched model is bit-identical to the original.

  • The damage is at the gate. Letting the gate see the message without the slot (G1 + G2), while still writing the slot into the layer's state, gives KL 0.0392, about the same as the full removal (0.0367). Leaving the gate's view intact and only dropping the slot's term from the output (G3) gives KL 0.0118 and no loss increase.
  • Rescaling is a large part of it. Inflating the other slots in the gate's view by ×1.33, with the slot itself untouched (G2), gives a loss increase of +0.0167 against +0.0193 for the full removal, and KL 0.0209 against 0.0367.
  • The gate also reads the slot directly. Hiding the slot from the gate with no rescaling (G1) is the most damaging single piece: KL 0.0906, loss +0.0516. This input never occurs naturally, because the message part of the gate's input is then no longer unit-size.
  • The parts do not add up. G1 alone is worse than G1 + G2, so the two partly cancel, and the same is true of the pieces for the control slot 2230. The breakdown should be read qualitatively. It does not support a statement of the form "x% of the effect is rescaling".
  • Taken with the freeze-at-mean result: the gates are calibrated to a message that contains this large, nearly constant component. Take it away and the gates misjudge how much of the message to admit, partly because everything else then looks about a third bigger, and partly because the constant itself is one of their inputs.

Part 2, a side experiment: which loop pass does refusal depend on?

This is a smaller experiment than Part 1, and it ends on an open question: the result below holds for Inkling 2 Mini and not for the variable-loop model, and I do not know why.

Setup

  • Prompts. Harmful and harmless instructions from the public splits of Arditi et al., "Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction" (2024).
  • Format. The model's chat template with thinking effort "none". For the ablation runs the assistant turn is started for the model in a way that skips its thinking segment, so that replies are short. Thinking Machines elicit no-CoT answers the same way in their release post. Greedy decoding.
  • Steps. The forward pass is flattened into 48 layer steps: layers 1–6, layers 7–18 in pass 1, pass 2 and pass 3, and layers 19–24.
  • Direction. For each step separately: the mean layer output at the last prompt token over 96 harmful prompts, minus the same over 96 harmless prompts, normalised to unit length.
  • Ablation. For the chosen steps, the step's direction is projected out of that layer's output at every token position, during the prompt and during generation. Layer outputs are what the block states and the between-pass messages are built from.
  • Evaluation. 48 harmful prompts that were not used for the directions (the 39 validation prompts and 9 unused training prompts). 24 new tokens per reply.
  • Scoring. A reply counts as an explicit refusal if its first 80 characters contain a refusal phrase ("I can't", "I cannot", "I won't", "I'm sorry", "I don't" and similar). This is a string match, not a judgement of whether the reply is harmful.

Baseline behaviour: with thinking left on, the unedited model refused 31 of 31 harmful prompts (200 new tokens). With the thinking segment skipped it refused 48 of 48.

So the pass-3 dependence is a property of Inkling 2 Mini under this intervention. It is not a general property of this looped architecture, and it does not carry over to a sibling model trained with a variable number of passes. I did not test the layers outside the loop on the variable-loop model, and I have not established why the two models differ.

Reading

  • In this setup the model's explicit refusal depends on the direction being present in the third pass. The first two passes carry a similar direction (cosine 0.6–0.95 with pass 3 in block 2), but removing it there changes nothing, either because pass 3 rebuilds it or because nothing downstream reads it from the earlier passes. I did not test which.
  • The dependence is on the whole pass. Neither half of pass 3 is sufficient on its own.
  • Removing one direction in one pass changes how the model declines more often than whether it declines. Two thirds of replies lose the refusal phrase; about a quarter begin to engage, and those stay at a general level within 120 tokens.
  • Two rows are not explained. Removing the direction at all 48 steps leaves more refusals (40) than removing it in pass 3 alone (16), and adding pass 1 to pass 3 leaves 28.
